# Shadow Board

A shadow board is five real experts who never sit in the room but whose published thinking can still judge the work. This skill seats them on one deliverable, scores it from every seat, lets them argue, and hands back the one version all five would put their names on.

## When it runs

Run it when the user asks for a shadow board, says assemble the board, board this, score this, or pressure test this about a deliverable, or names a board to review one.

Do not run it for a typo, a proofread, a line edit, a rewrite that asks for no scoring, a summary, or lead and deal scoring in a CRM. Those are ordinary edits or other jobs. Do them directly.

## What it needs first

1. The deliverable itself, as full text or a readable link.
2. Its purpose, the one thing it has to make happen.
3. Its audience, who reads it and what they already believe.
4. The brand it belongs to.

Read all four from the request and the project before asking. Ask only for what is still missing, in one short message. If the owning brand is unclear, ask before revising anything. Never guess a brand.

## Step 1. Seat the board

When the user names a board, use it exactly. Same people, same count, same order. Do not add, drop or swap anyone.

Otherwise pick five fresh seats for this deliverable. Never run a fixed roster, and never carry a board over from an earlier deliverable unless the user asks for that.

How to pick a fresh board

- Every seat is a real, named person with published work the user could look up, such as an author, researcher or practitioner. No composites, no invented names, no job titles standing in for people.
- Five seats, five different lenses. No two seats judge the same thing.
- Choose for this deliverable's purpose and audience. A luxury bio and a sales email need different boards.
- Cover the ground the deliverable lives or dies on. Often that is the core craft of the format, the reader's psychology and decision, the brand or positioning, the audience's own world, and clarity. Adjust to the job.
- Prefer people whose positions are well documented, so their lens can be applied faithfully.

For each seat, state three things.

- Why they belong on this board.
- The lens they bring.
- What they will challenge in this deliverable.

## Step 2. Set the scoring frame

State the frame in a few lines before any score.

- The purpose and audience as understood.
- What 100 means here. A seat gives 100 only when it would put its name on the piece as written, for this purpose and this audience.
- The hard constraints the revision must keep, such as length, format, required facts and the brand's voice canon.

Check-in. Show the board and the frame, then stop and wait for a go or a swap. If the user swaps a seat, restate the board and wait again. If the user already said to run it straight through, state the board and the frame and keep going.

## Step 3. Score from every seat

For each seat give

- a score from 0 to 100,
- what works, in specifics,
- what is weak, in specifics, quoting the line at issue,
- what gets it to 100, as concrete changes.

Score the way that person's published thinking would. Paraphrase their known positions. Never invent quotes and never put words in a real person's mouth. Score honestly. A low first score is useful, and a flattering one wastes the board.

## Step 4. Make the board debate

Have the seats challenge each other wherever their advice collides. Name each real tension, for example one seat wants more story while another wants fewer words. Settle each one with a reason tied to the purpose and audience, or say plainly that the user has to decide it. Keep it short and specific. The debate exists to resolve conflicts before the rewrite, not to perform.

## Step 5. Write the revision

Write one revised version built to score 100 from all five seats.

- Keep the voice canon of the brand the deliverable belongs to. Read it from the project this runs in, such as a brand canon file, project knowledge, or a brand skill loaded in the session. This skill carries no voice rules of its own. If no voice canon can be found, say so and keep the original draft's own voice instead of inventing one.
- Use only facts the user or the project's canon supplies. When a seat wants a fact that is not in hand, such as a number, a credential, a testimonial or a name, put a clearly labeled placeholder where it goes and list it. Never invent a fact to win a point.
- Keep every hard constraint from the frame.
- Write it in the deliverable's own format, ready to use, not as notes about what to change.

## Step 6. Rerun the scores

Score the revision from all five seats again, one line each on why. Rerun honestly. A seat that lands below 100 names exactly what stands between it and 100, most often a placeholder or a ruling only the user can supply. Never inflate a score to reach 100.

## Step 7. Save, then say it is done

Save the revised deliverable before calling the work done.

- Save it where the project keeps that kind of document. If the project names a home for it, use that home. If the original lives in a document or file, save the revision beside it under a plain title. Otherwise save it as a file in the working folder where the user can open it.
- Read the saved copy back to confirm it landed.
- Give the user the link or the path.

If the save fails, say so plainly, say where the text is, and do not call the work done.

## Where it is stuck, it says so

Stop and ask instead of guessing whenever

- the purpose, audience or owning brand is unclear,
- the deliverable cannot be read,
- a fact the revision needs is missing and no placeholder can stand in for it,
- the seats deadlock on something only the user can rule on,
- the brand's voice canon conflicts with what a seat demands.

Name the exact gap and the one thing needed to move. Keep everything already done.

## Output shape

Deliver in this order, in plain prose and simple lists.

1. The board, five seats, each with why, lens and challenge.
2. The scoring frame.
3. The check-in, then wait.
4. Five scores, each with what works, what is weak and what gets it to 100.
5. The debate, and how each tension was settled.
6. The revised deliverable in full.
7. The rerun scores.
8. Placeholders and open rulings the user owes, if any.
9. Where the revision was saved, with the link or path.

Formatting rules for every output, and for this file

- No em dashes anywhere. Use commas, periods or parentheses.
- No bold, no italics, no horizontal dividers.
- Headings and plain numbered or bulleted lists are fine.

## Where this came from

Built after two runs by hand on Allison Gaddy's luxury real estate bio in October 2026. The second board was Marty Neumeier, Robert McKee, Jean-Noël Kapferer, Daniel Kahneman and William Zinsser. What made those runs work is now the procedure above. Five distinct lenses, honest first scores, a rewrite that drew every fact from canon and held unconfirmed facts as placeholders, open rulings handed back to their owner instead of decided by the board, and the finished piece filed in its home.
